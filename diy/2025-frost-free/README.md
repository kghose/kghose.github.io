# Upgrading to frost free sillcocks

Both front and rear hosebibs are leaking from the valve stem. Let's take this
opportunity to upgrade to frost free sillcocks. 

## Before

![](original-hosebib-rear-exterior.jpg) 

The original hose bibs looked like this on the outside. Sure we could repack the
nuts, but where's the fun in that? And frost free upgrade, remember?


![](original-hosebib-rear-interior.jpg) 

This is the plumbing for the rear hosebib. The piping around the shut off gate
valve looks cruddy, but it's just a bit of flux, and the gate valve and pipes
work just fine. I think all I had to do was replace the washer on the nipple cap
like ten years ago (You can see the cap poking out at the base of the valve). If

![](original-hosebib-front-interior-1.jpg)
![](original-hosebib-front-interior-2.jpg)

This is the plumbing for the front hosebib. Again, pipes and gate valve work
just fine.

The winterization process for both bibs is the same:

1. Shut off the gate valve.
1. Open the hosebib valve.
1. Place a cup under the gate valve and loosen the nipple.
1. Collect the runoff water
1. Tighten the nipple.

It's a chore to do this. You need to get on a step-stool to reach the nipple cap
and place the cup under it. The gate valve for the front hosebib is jammed up
against a wall. You need to walk down a narrow alley in the basement with barely
enough room to open the step-stool. You need to use a pair of needle-nose pliers
to operate the drain cap because it's pointed into the top plate of the wall
(Look at the photo again - the plumber had to cut a corner off the top plate to
fit the gate valve there).


## The plan

Let's replace these leaking 60yo antique spigots held together with caulk and
layers of lead paint (thank you for your service) with shiny modern frost-free
hydrants. 

![](c-134d10-3.jpg)

> [!WARNING]
> As I was looking through the spec list for a hydrant I was considering I read:
> **"Not intended for continuous use."** 
>
> What's that about? My intended use was to leave the hydrant on all the time,
> connected to a timer, which would operate a sprinkler system. That won't work. 
>
> Turns out many hydrants now have anti-siphon valves builtin. These valves
> prevent dirty water from being sucked back from the garden into the potable
> water system via siphoning. These valves are rated for only 12 hrs of
> continuous operation.
> 
> I had to look specifically for a hydrant that didn't have this valve built in.
> For safety I will add such backflow valves separately to the pipes after the
> hydrant and the timer valve. 

Frost-free hydrants have long pipes and though the wheel is near the mouth of
the hydrant as usual, the actual valve is all the way at the end of that pipe.
The flange which attaches the hydrant to the wall is designed to pitch the pipe
up. 

When you shut off the hydrant with the wheel, the valve all the way in the back
closes. Because the pipe slopes down, all the water in the pipe drains out of
the mouth, leaving the pipe empty. 

The end of the pipe is so long, it sits inside the house, in the conditioned
space which doesn't freeze (unless some other disaster has happened in the
house) so every time you close the hydrant no water remains in the part of the
pipe that could freeze.

Winterization will longer involve the yearly solstice ritual of the cup and the
step-ladder and the needle nose pliers and the dancing in the narrow alley. You
just remove the hose from the hydrant. The promise land beckons.

If we were a real plumber we would be sweating the pipe and using solder joints
everywhere, but we aren't. Despite the name of the site, we do our very very
best to avoid intentional fires, so we will be using Sharkbite (push-to-connect)
fittings.


## Doing the rear hosebib


I used one of these fabulous close quarters pipe cutters to remove the spigot.

![](rear-piping-cut.jpg)

These cutters make neat perpendicular cuts and need very little maneuvering
space, or muscle power.

![](rear-siding-step.jpg)

The first surprise in this project: possibly original wood siding underneath the
vinyl rather than plywood and there is an overlap right where we don’t want it.

![](rear-siding-shim.jpg)

A million to one shot doc. I had a piece of wood lying around. If I recall
correctly, it was from the broken slat from some old IKEA sofa bed, squirreled
away for twenty years. Packrats to the rescue! It was the exact right thickness,
so I sawed it to size, cut a slot and screwed it in place. 

I later had to add a second piece below: the siding was pushing down on the
block and bending it, so I needed the shim to cover the _entire_ vinyl siding
block.

![](rear-siding-block.jpg)

I was too conservative with my cuts and needed to trim a lot afterwards. This
was a good use of the old multi-tool.


![](rear-piping-marking-for-cut.jpg)

Lining up the two pipes and marking off where to cut, remembering to allow 1"
for the Sharkbite fitting. We will remember to use fine grit sandpaper to
smoothen out the outside and inside of the pipe, and use the Sharkbite deburring
tool to deburr the cut edge and to mark off the proper insertion depth. 

The hydrant has a threaded end (Male National Pipe Thread) which I have wrapped
with teflon tape and screwed a Sharkbite threaded + push-to-connect adapter on
to.

![](rear-piping-sharkbite-connected.jpg)

The Sharkbite connection is anti-climactic. Just push it in! The last bit does
need a surprising amount of force and goes home with a thud. Thankfully we have
the reference mark from the depth gauge to guide us.

![](rear-outside-hydrant-attached.jpg)

I have a truckload of these #8 GRK cabinet screws that I use everywhere. I used
them to attach the hydrant flange to the house. Looking good.

![](rear-piping-threaded-leaking.jpg)

A little post script. I pressurized the circuit and watched it for a bit. After
half an hour this little bead formed. The bead formed on the threaded joint
(Sharkbite to hydrant) rather than the push-fit joint (Sharkbite to copper).

I recalled then that I had only hand tightened this threaded joint. I used a
pair of wrenches to give it a quarter turn which fixed the beading.

It is very fortunate that Sharkbites can be rotated. If they couldn’t this
wouldn’t have been a postscript. It would have been an annoying learning moment.

## Front hydrant 

This one was much more "interesting" than the rear one. Let's start with an
opening question.

### Copper or Lead?

![](front-piping-copper-or-lead-1.jpg)

What kind of pipe is this? It’s blackish in appearance and non-magnetic. 

![](front-piping-copper-or-lead-2.jpg)

I scratched it lightly with a coin. It looked dull gray to me.

OK. This is lead pipe? I should probably leave it alone?

I asked around on an online forum and posted a few pics. Someone replied that I
should scratch some more because they were sure it was copper pipe and further
more all the fittings visible were copper fittings.

So I used a bit of sandpaper on the pipes.

![](front-piping-copper-or-lead-3.jpg)

Is the sandpapered bit coppery or silvery? 

![](front-piping-copper-or-lead-4.jpg)

There was some debate, but in the end the consensus was that this was indeed
Copper pipe.

I did some searches online and learned that cold water copper pipes in humid
environments can turn blackish over time. Maybe that is it?

![](front-piping-cut.jpg)

Proceeding on the assumption that this is Copper pipe, I estimated how deep the
end of the new hydrant would reach, left myself plenty of margin, cut the pipe,
and removed the old spigot. So far so good.

![](front-siding-cutout.jpg)

I didn't take many photos of the outside carpentry work. It was pleasant in the
fair weather and went reasonably fast as I had gained experience from the
iteration with the rear hydrant. 

I enlarged the existing hole vertically all the way to the sill plate. I knew
the new hydrant would pitch downwards, so I wanted to make sure the back end of
the hydrant wouldn't rise above the end of the supply pipe. 

There was wooden siding here too, but the overlap was above the hydrant
location. In my first try I cut away the wood overlapping on the top and
placed the vinyl block against the lower layer but this set the block too low:
The siding all around it kept popping out the trim. 

I cut a 1x4 to the same length as the vinyl block and placed the vinyl utility
block on that. It worked fabulously, though now I had ¾" less hydrant to work
with inside.

I also got to see a 1x4 board crack when a screw was put through it. I cut a 1"
hole in the board for the hydrant to pass through and then I put in a #8 screw
to attach the bottom of the board to the house, no problem. 

Then I put a #10 screw through the top and it split the board right to the
through hole. I guess the #10 put enough stress that the weakening from the 1”
hole caused it to finally crack.


![](new-front-exterior.jpg)

All said and done, we can do this outside part in our sleep now.

Looking good!


### I've got a bad feeling about this

![](new-front-interior-flawed-joint.jpg)

I had juuust enough pipe to prepare everything for a straight up shark bite
connection. Having learned from my previous iteration, I had already wrench
tightened the threaded connection and look, the pressure test succeeds! All
done, right? Right?

So why am I not happy?

Because the pipes look unhappy.\
Because the hydrant behaves wrong. 

![](original-hosebib-front-interior-1.jpg)

It's hard to tell from the "before" photos because the wide angle of the phone
camera distorts the perspective, but the original line pitched upwards as it
went out. This worked OK with the old winterization process but clashes with the
new piping.

The frost free hydrant pipe is designed to pitch downwards, opposite what the
supply pipe wants to do. If you look again at the current join, you can see the
two sections want to do opposite things, and the Sharkbite fitting is the focal
point of this tension.

Additionally, there was the behavior of the hydrant. These frost free hydrants
run a bit after you close the valve. This is the water draining from the long
throat (which is what makes it frost free). 

I had experience of just how much water came out from the 12" long hydrant I
just installed. The drainage from this one was just a trickle. Even though it
was shorter (8") it still felt far too little. 

![](new-front-interior-piping-offset-1.jpg)
![](new-front-interior-piping-offset-2.jpg)

I was bothered enough by these thoughts running through my head as I settled in
for the night, that I got out of bed, went down to the basement and cut the
pipe. 

The two parts immediately sprang apart laterally. I’d say they were 1/4" inch
out of alignment, and wanting to pitch in opposite ways.

OK. Intuition vindicated. What’s the fix here? What’s the plan?

I have to bridge this offset (and pitch mismatch) ...

![](new-front-interior-piping-gap.jpg)

... within a distance of less than 2"

If I were a real plumber, I'd have a pipe spring and a bending jig, and I'd take
a section of copper pipe, put it in the jig with the spring through it and then
bend it into a gentle "S" shape to exactly match this requirement and then sweat
it in and it would be a piece of art.

But I'm just an amateur DIY guy learning about pipes.

I decide that an inverted U is the best I can do. I could buy a pocketfull of
Sharkbite elbows and play around with bits of copper pipe to assemble a U, but I
decide that the mismatching pitches makes this unsolvable with rigid pipe.

### I guess I'm learning PEX

![](pex-iteration-1.jpg)

I guess I’m going to learn how to do plumbing with PEX.

After some internet research I decided to go with PEX B and cinch clamps.

Like working with Sharkbites, it’s so easy, easier even, that I keep thinking
this can’t possibly work in real life


![](pex-iteration-1-dry-fit.jpg)

This is my first design. 

The dry fit suggests some tweaking of the design is needed. That left arm needs
to be a tad longer and the U needs to be slightly narrower.

![](pex-iteration-2.jpg)

Just toss the old sections and cut new ones.

![](pex-iteration-2-dry-fit.jpg)

Dry fit looked good, so let's cinch things up.

![](pex-iteration-2-beading.jpg)

Pressure test reveals beading on threaded joint. I guess threaded joints are my
weakness, literally. I guess what feels wrench tight to me, is not wrench tight
to more muscular real plumbers.

I know what you're thinking. "Did he make two turns after finger tight, or only
one?" Well to tell you the truth in all this excitement I kinda lost track
myself.

I kept tightening till I was worried that I was stripping threads. Still the
beading persisted.

I’d have to redo the threaded joint. Because there was so little space, I'd have
to disconnect the U to do this. I'd have to remove the PEX clamp holding the
last section of the U to the threaded elbow, but I'd have to get rid of that
entire section, so I'd have to remove the clamp on the elbow before that too.

It was about this point that I began to feel burnt out. Like most DIY people I
was doing this late at night when all other duties were done, and I was starting
to feel hopelessness. 

This was mostly due to lack of sleep several nights in a row for unrelated
reasons. 

I was able to identify this issue and stop work. Fortunately this was not an
urgent project and I had a dedicated shutoff for this line, so I had this
luxury.

I tackled it again next night with renewed enthusiasm after reading up a bit
about [threaded joints]. I assembled the joint, carefully noting the finger
tight position, being mindful about the layers of teflon tape (I did four turns
this time), and tightening it one and a half full turns beyond the dry fit
position.

[threaded joints]: /plumbing#threaded-joints

![](pex-iteration-2-removal-hair-dryer.jpg)

I learned how to [remove a cinch clamp] and figured out that a hair dryer was
the simplest and least unpredictable way of [removing the pipe] from the fitting
afterwards.

[remove a cinch clamp]: /plumbing/remove-pex-b-cinch-clamps
[removing the pipe]: /plumbing/remove-pex-b-from-fittings

I’m learning new stuff and kicking ass. I’m back in a growth mindset and in a
good mood.

It is true that you learn the most from making mistakes. I just needed some good
night’s sleep to get to that perspective.

![](pex-iteration-3.jpg)

That last cinch clamp (on the threaded elbow)  gives me uneasiness - between the
concrete sill and the other pipes, the place is cramped enough that I can’t get
the cinch tool to close in one go: I close the cinch part way, have to
de-ratched the jaws, then come in at a different angle and complete the
compression.

![](pex-iteration-3-zoomed-out.jpg)

Finally, at 12:30 am, I think we are done. The pressure test passes!

The design has too many joints for my taste, but it’s in a corner I can check
easily, and if the joints leak, I will spend the time to come up with a solution
that involves fewer joints.

### What would an ideal solution look like?

There is that vertical pipe (the main supply) which has a T on it which goes to
a horizontal elbow which goes to the gate valve.

Without being able to sweat copper, I would probably try and get a Sharkbite
elbow valve to replace that first sweated elbow. There is a question of if I'll
have enough pipe off that T though.

I would keep the elbow oriented horizontally. The new gap (~12") is probably
enough for PEX to bend and adjust to the offset and pitch difference. Probably. 


## Lessons reinforced

1. If your instinct tells you something is wrong, check it!
1. Take the time to do it right!
1. If you burnout, take a break
